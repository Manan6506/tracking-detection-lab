Tracking Detection Lab

What a website — and the trackers inside it — learn about you the moment you arrive.

This is a live educational demo. It shows you the data that any web server receives about you automatically, plus the browser fingerprint a tracker could build. Nothing is stored and nothing is sent to third parties — see Privacy. It demonstrates tracking so you can understand and defend against it.

5 How to reduce what they get

None of this makes you invisible, but each step cuts down the signal.

Hide or change your IP

  • A VPN replaces your IP with the VPN server’s, so IP geolocation points at the server, not you.
  • iCloud Private Relay (Safari, paid iCloud) hides your IP from the sites you visit.
  • Tor Browser routes through several relays, so sites see an exit node far away.

Resist fingerprinting

  • Tor Browser and Firefox (with “resist fingerprinting”) make many users look identical.
  • Brave randomises canvas/WebGL readings.
  • Keeping your browser stock (no rare fonts or extensions that alter pages) makes you blend in — ironically, a very customised browser is easier to single out.

Cut the trackers themselves

  • uBlock Origin or built-in blocking (Brave, Firefox, Safari) stops much ad/analytics code from loading at all.
  • Block third-party cookies — Safari and Firefox do by default.
  • Watch this lab’s effect: run it with a blocker on, and the IP lookup may be blocked too.

6 How it works

Why every server sees your IP

For a server to send a page back, the request must carry a return address — your public IP. It’s part of how the internet routes traffic, so no permission could be asked for it without breaking the web. Any server you contact sees it: the website, and every third party whose code the site embeds.

Companies map IP ranges to places using registry records and measurements. That’s “IP geolocation”: fast, free, no consent, and only city-accurate.

How one tracker sees you across many sites

A site doesn’t sell your data by emailing it. Instead it embeds an ad or analytics company’s script or pixel. That third party’s code runs on thousands of sites, so the same company receives a request — with your IP, a cookie or fingerprint, and the Referer page — from each site you visit. Stitching those together is how a profile forms.

This demo deliberately can’t do that: it only sees this one page, and it embeds no real trackers.

Cookies vs. fingerprints

A cookie is an ID the tracker stores in your browser; you can clear it or block it. A fingerprint isn’t stored — it’s recomputed from your device’s traits each visit, so clearing cookies doesn’t shake it. That’s why your original brief’s concern about fingerprinting matters: it’s the fallback when cookies fail.

What a page still cannot do

  • Read your precise location without the permission prompt in section 4.
  • List your Wi-Fi networks, scan your home network, or read other devices.
  • See your other tabs, your history, or your files (beyond ones you choose).
  • Get your name or exact address from your IP alone.

7 What this demo itself does

  • No storage, no profiles kept. The server reads your request, replies to you, and forgets. There is no database and no visitor log in this code.
  • No third-party trackers. The Content Security Policy blocks every connection except to this site itself, the GeoJS IP database, and OpenStreetMap for the map. You can confirm this in your browser’s network tools.
  • The fingerprint stays with you. It is computed in your browser and shown only on your screen; this code never transmits it.
  • External services contacted: GeoJS (get.geojs.io) receives your IP to return a city, and only when you are not on localhost; OpenStreetMap receives coordinates to draw the map. Each has its own privacy policy.
  • When you deploy it for teammates, they will each see their own data on their own screen. Tell them it’s a tracking demo — that honesty is the difference between teaching and surveillance.
